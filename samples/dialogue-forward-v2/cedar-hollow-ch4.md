The sound of the lake lapping against the shore is the only thing that breaks the silence. You're standing at the edge of the Cedar Hollow cemetery, the last light of dusk painting the pines in bruised purples and grays. The air is cool, carrying the scent of damp earth and pine needles. You came to see if his truck was still here, and it is. Parked a little crooked, like he was in a hurry.

You see him then, a dark silhouette against the deepening twilight, a few rows in. He's standing by a headstone, head bowed. Eli Crane. The name engraved on the stone is faded, but you know it by heart: "Thomas Crane." His father. The man who taught you both how to skip stones, how to bait a hook.

He says something, a low murmur carried on the breeze, too soft to make out. Then another, a little louder, and you catch a few words, fragmented, raw: "…the dock… and she's back… I should have…" His voice cracks, a rough, uncharacteristic sound. The careful, capable man you've known your whole life, undone by grief. He brings a hand up, rubbing at his eyes with the heel of his palm, and the gesture is so entirely unguarded, so painfully human, that a strange, unfamiliar ache settles in your chest. This isn't the Eli Crane who sits on the town council, who coolly deflects your every argument. This is something else entirely. Something real.

You step back, further into the cover of the old cedars, the needles cushioning your footsteps. He doesn't see you. You let him have his moment, a quiet witness to a grief he has never let this town see. You shouldn't have heard any of it. But you did. And now, you can't un-hear it.

You're walking back along the narrow cemetery path, the gravel crunching under your boots, when he appears from behind a stand of weeping willows. He stops short, a hand still in his pocket, the other running through his already disheveled hair. His eyes, usually so steady and unreadable, have a faint redness around them. He's seen you, then. There's no point in pretending otherwise.

"Lost?" he asks, his voice a little rougher than usual. He doesn't look away, not quite, but his gaze slides just past your shoulder.

"Just… taking a walk," you reply, the lie feeling thin and sharp in the quiet air. You meet his eyes directly, and for a split second, you see it there – the knowledge that you saw. The vulnerability. It flickers and then it's gone, replaced by the familiar shield.

He nods, slow and deliberate. "Right. The lakehouse isn't far from here." He shoves his hands deeper into his pockets, and you notice how his forearms are more than you remembered, the fabric of his flannel straining faintly. "Everything alright over there? No new disasters with the dock?"

"No new disasters, thanks to your… thorough inspection," you say, a dry note in your voice. You don't want to talk about the dock. Or the lakehouse. Or anything that feels like business. "Just enjoying the… local scenery."

He lets out a low, humorless laugh. "Cedar Hollow's particular brand of scenery, I suppose." He takes a step closer, and the scent of pine and something distinctly masculine, his own scent, reaches you. "Didn't expect to see you out here after dark. Most people prefer the daylight tours."

"I'm not 'most people,' Eli," you tell him, the words coming out sharper than you intended. "And I like a little quiet now and then. Something you might appreciate, too." You gesture vaguely toward the silent rows of headstones around them.

He holds your gaze then, truly holds it, and for a moment, the air feels thick, charged with unspoken things. He looks like he wants to say something, something just below the surface of the dry banter. His jaw works, a slight tremor in his hand as he finally pulls it from his pocket to adjust the cuff of his flannel.

"Right," he says, the word tight. "Well. Try not to trip over any ancestors on your way back." He turns to leave, but then he pauses, his back to you. "Look. About the council meeting next week…" His voice is quieter now, almost hesitant. "The dock isn't the only thing we need to discuss." He doesn't elaborate, doesn't turn around. He just leaves the words hanging in the cool night air, a question mark on the edge of the cemetery, and then he's gone, disappearing between the shadowed headstones.
